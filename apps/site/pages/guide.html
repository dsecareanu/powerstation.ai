Power infrastructure for the AI era

AI runs on electricity. In many places, power is now harder to secure than the chips. This guide covers how much electricity AI needs, why grid connections take so long, and what developers, utilities and power producers are building.

How much power AI needs

Data centers have long used a steady, small share of electricity. AI changes this. Training and running large models needs dense racks of GPUs and other accelerators. Each rack draws much more power than a rack of ordinary servers. The largest new campuses are planned at hundreds of megawatts, and some at more than a gigawatt.

The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates that data centers used about 415 TWh in 2024. That is about 1.5% of the world's electricity. In its base case, the IEA expects about 945 TWh by 2030, with AI as the main driver. Source: IEA, "Energy and AI", 2025.

World averages hide where the pressure is. Demand gathers in a few regions where land, fiber and power are all available. There, one campus can use as much power as a mid-sized city. Utilities that planned for flat demand now have to plan for fast growth.

Why the grid is the bottleneck

A data center can be built in one to two years. The power connection for it often takes longer.

Connection queues

New large loads and new power plants must be studied and approved before they connect. In many markets, these queues now take years. So developers often choose a site by where power is available, not by where land or customers are.

Transmission lines and equipment

New transmission lines take a long time to get permits. Transformers, switchgear and turbines take a long time to make, and supply has not kept up with orders.

Power every hour

AI training needs steady power in every hour of the year. Firm sources can supply this. Wind and solar can too, when they are paired with storage or backup. Grid operators and regulators also have to decide how the cost of grid upgrades for one very large customer is shared.

Where the power will come from

Power sources for AI loads
Source Role for AI loads Typical time to add
Grid upgrades and existing plants Lowest cost where spare capacity exists. Limited by queues and transmission. Now to several years
Natural gas, often on site Firm power that is fast to build. Emissions and turbine supply are the limits. 1 to 3 years
Solar and wind with batteries Low-cost energy. Needs storage or backup for loads that run day and night. 1 to 3 years
Existing nuclear plants (restarts, upgrades, long contracts) Firm, low-carbon power, bought under long-term contracts. Now to a few years
Small modular reactors (SMRs) Firm, low-carbon power that can sit near a campus. Not yet built at commercial scale. Mostly the 2030s
Geothermal and other firm low-carbon power Newer options, often backed by long-term purchase deals with large tech companies. Late 2020s onward

The largest cloud companies follow a similar pattern. They sign long-term power purchase agreements for whatever can deliver soonest. They also invest in firm, low-carbon sources for the next decade. Deals to restart a closed reactor and to buy power from SMR developers are now part of this mix.

Some campuses produce power on site or next to the site, "behind the meter", instead of drawing it from the grid. This used to be rare. It is now a common option where a grid connection is slow.

Using less: cooling, chips and flexibility

Liquid cooling
Direct-to-chip and immersion cooling can handle racks that are too dense for air cooling. They also use less energy for cooling.
More efficient chips
Each new generation of accelerators does more work per watt. Total demand still rises, because use grows faster.
Flexible load
Some AI work, such as training and batch jobs, can slow down or move when the grid is under stress. Some grid operators now treat flexible data centers as a resource. This can make a faster connection possible.
Heat reuse
Waste heat can feed district heating networks where these networks exist.

Who is building it

Power for AI is becoming an industry of its own. Several groups work on it:

  • Data center developers and large cloud companies, who secure land and power together, often years ahead.
  • Utilities and grid operators, who update load forecasts, connection rules and tariffs for very large loads.
  • Independent power producers, who build or convert power plants next to data center campuses.
  • Nuclear and SMR developers, who sign long-term supply deals with technology companies.
  • Makers of turbines, transformers, batteries, cooling and power electronics, who are growing their supply.
  • "Power-first" AI campuses: companies that start with a power source and build computing around it.

Questions and answers

How much electricity do data centers use?

The IEA estimates about 415 TWh in 2024, about 1.5% of the world's electricity. It expects about 945 TWh by 2030, with AI as the main driver (IEA, "Energy and AI", 2025).

Why is the grid connection the bottleneck for AI data centers?

A large AI campus can need hundreds of megawatts or more, in every hour. New transmission lines, substations and power plants take years to approve and build. In many markets, the wait for a connection is now longer than the time to build the data center.

Will nuclear power supply AI data centers?

Partly, over time. Large cloud companies have signed long-term deals to restart or extend existing reactors, and to buy power from small modular reactors. Most new SMR capacity is not expected before the 2030s. Until then, gas, renewables with storage, and grid upgrades supply most of the new demand.
